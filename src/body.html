AI in the Research Process

For researchers and doctoral candidates: where AI genuinely helps along the research process – and where it does not. 67 use cases from the first idea to transfer, each with a starter prompt, a check and practice data. The guides work with any general AI assistant your university provides or permits (e.g. Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini).

How this tool works

Two steps: first decide what is worth it for your research, then try it – on your own or with a group in a workshop.

Step 1

Research value

Enter your discipline and go through the phases of the research process from that point of view: how valuable for your research, what kind of gain, how feasible. Star what you want to try, flag what is “not an AI task” or needs a rules check, and add missing use cases.

→ Results: the value × feasibility matrix shows which use cases win.

Step 2

Try it with AI

Pick one to three use cases and test them with your AI assistant – with a short guide, a starter prompt, a check and the practice data. Climb the levels from a single chat to a project, a custom assistant and, where it adds something, automation or your own code. Record how it went and what you noticed.

Alone or in a workshop

  • On your own: just start – ratings, tests and findings are saved in this browser only.
  • In a workshop: each group works on one laptop and gives it a group name in the menu (gear icon, top right). Afterwards each group copies its data and one laptop merges them – Results then compares groups and disciplines.

Before you upload anything

  • Use the assistant your university provides or permits, signed in with your university account where possible.
  • Personal, confidential or unpublished data only go into approved tools – otherwise use the practice data or anonymised material.
  • Check every number, quote and reference against the source. You remain responsible for the result and for disclosing AI use.

How to use it with an AI assistant

  1. Download papers and data and unzip the pack – or download single files below.
  2. Pick a test idea below, or any paper or data file that fits your use case.
  3. In a chat with your AI assistant, upload the paper or data file you need for your prompt.
  4. For a data file, first ask the assistant to describe it: columns, units, missing-value codes. Check this against the file.
  5. Then run your use case – and check every number, quote and citation against the data or the PDF.

Assistants often read a PDF as text, so tables, figures and equations are easily misread – check them in the paper itself. If your assistant does not accept a .txt file, rename it to .csv or paste its content into the chat.

    Academic Extensions & Tools

    Tools commonly used in academic work, mapped to the research phases – for orientation, not recommendations. They complement a general AI assistant rather than replace it: subject databases, reference managers and analysis software do things a general assistant cannot. Many of them can also be connected to an assistant, most flexibly through the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard that lets an assistant call another tool and use its results.

    Example: an assistant connected to scite’s MCP server can check, while you draft a literature review, whether later studies support or contrast a finding you cite – and answers with the actual citation statements and DOIs instead of guessing.

    Ratings: by students at a German university, 2024/25 (greyed out with fewer than three ratings); tools without a rating were added later.     How to connect a tool to an AI assistant via MCPThree routes, from simple to flexible – and what to check first.

    1 · Ready-made connectors

    Most assistants offer ready-made connectors or apps – depending on the tool and licence, for example for scite, Consensus, PubMed, GitHub or Notion. Many of them use MCP in the background. Nothing to build – check which ones your university has enabled.

    2 · Your own assistant or automation

    1. Choose a platform that accepts MCP servers – for example the custom-connector option of your assistant, Microsoft Copilot Studio or a workflow tool such as n8n.
    2. Enter a name, a short description (the assistant uses it to decide when to call the tool) and the server URL from the provider’s documentation.
    3. Choose how to sign in: none, API key or OAuth 2.0. With OAuth, each researcher uses their own account.
    4. Add instructions such as “cite DOIs, never invent sources”, test with real examples, then share it with your team.

    3 · Code, desktop and local tools

    Code editors such as VS Code and desktop assistants connect to MCP servers directly – including local ones such as Zotero or jamovi that never leave your computer.

    Without MCP

    Many tools already work where you write: reference managers cite in Word (Zotero, EndNote, Mendeley, Citavi, Paperpile), note-takers join video meetings (Otter, Sally), and some offer automation connectors. Filter by “Microsoft 365 integration” below for the Microsoft route.

    Before you start

    • Cloud platforms only connect to remote servers (HTTPS). Local community servers need a code editor or a desktop assistant.
    • Connectors and custom assistants may need additional licences; IT may have to approve them and can restrict connectors.
    • Your prompts and inputs go to the tool provider. Check your university’s data rules; never send unpublished or personal data to an unvetted server.
    • Community servers are not maintained by the provider – review the code and permissions first.

    About AI assistants

    Use the assistant your university provides or permits – with a university account, inputs are usually covered by a data-protection agreement and not used for training.
