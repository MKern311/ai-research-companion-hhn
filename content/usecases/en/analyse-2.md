Propose a coding frame, do first-pass coding, suggest candidate themes and verify every quote – with human validation. Check whether this fits your approach: saturation checks belong to codebook or grounded-theory approaches, and reflexive thematic analysis is critical of AI coding.

## Problem

First-pass coding takes weeks, and alone it is hard to tell whether codes are applied consistently, whether themes are more than topic summaries, or when new data stop adding codes.

## Result

A draft coding frame with definitions and verbatim evidence that you test against your own reading – a sparring partner for coding that leaves interpretation and theme development with you.

## Steps

1. Practise on published or invented texts, never participant data
2. Ask for codes with definitions and verbatim quotes
3. Check that every quote exists and fits its code
4. Record where your reading differs and why

## Concepts

### Braun & Clarke reflexive thematic analysis

Six phases: familiarisation, coding, generating themes, reviewing, defining and naming, writing up (Braun & Clarke, 2006), named reflexive thematic analysis in 2019. Themes are patterns of shared meaning the researcher constructs, not topic summaries; as subjectivity is treated as a resource, AI output can only prompt your reflection.

### Grounded theory coding

Developed by Glaser and Strauss and later Charmaz: initial (open) coding close to the data, focused coding of the most significant codes, then theoretical coding, linked by constant comparison and memo-writing. Use it when building theory from data.

### Codes and themes; first- and second-cycle coding

A code labels a feature of a data segment; a theme is a broader pattern of meaning around a central concept. Saldaña distinguishes first-cycle methods (e.g. descriptive, in vivo) from second-cycle methods (e.g. pattern coding).

### Saturation

Code saturation – the point at which new data add no new codes – belongs to codebook and grounded-theory approaches. Guest et al. (2006) found most codes within the first twelve interviews of a fairly homogeneous sample, not a universal rule; grounded theory speaks of theoretical saturation of categories. Braun and Clarke (2021) reject saturation for reflexive thematic analysis, where meaning is generated by the researcher, not found in the data.

## Practice exercise

### Task

Treat four attribution studies as documents and code how they communicate uncertainty and limitations. You learn whether the assistant quotes verbatim with correct pages, stays within the text and keeps its codes distinct.

### Prompt

```text
I am practising qualitative document analysis. Research question: how do climate attribution studies communicate uncertainty and limitations to their readers? Attached are four PDFs: Christidis et al. (2020), Christidis et al. (2023), Zachariah et al. (2023) and Schumacher et al. (2024). Use only each paper's abstract and its discussion or conclusions section. Propose a coding frame of 5–7 codes, each with a one-line definition and one example quote. Then code each paper, giving the exact passage and page number for every code applied. Suggest how the codes might group into 2–3 candidate themes. As a code-saturation check of the kind used in codebook approaches, say whether the fourth paper still produced new codes. Point out passages that could be read in more than one way. Quote word for word and do not go beyond what the texts say.
```

### Check

Search every quote in the PDFs with Ctrl+F; it must appear word for word on the stated page. Note at least one code you would define differently and one passage you would code differently. The saturation question fits codebook approaches only; in reflexive thematic analysis, read the themes as prompts for your own thinking.

## Own material

### Bring

Two or three short invented interview excerpts, or quotes from an open-access qualitative paper, and your research question; never your participants' data.

### Steps

1. Paste the starter prompt with your research question and the excerpts.
2. Check that each code has a clear definition and a fitting example quote.
3. Ask how the codes group into candidate themes; in a codebook approach, also ask whether the last excerpt still added new codes.
4. Compare with your own reading and note what the assistant missed, flattened or over-interpreted.

### Prompt

```text
I am a qualitative researcher in [discipline]. My research question is: [question]. Below are [number] short excerpts for practice. Propose a coding frame of 5–8 codes, each with a one-line definition and an example quote. Then code each excerpt, showing the exact passage for every code applied. Finally, suggest how the codes might group into 2–3 candidate themes and point out where an excerpt could be read in more than one way. [Only for codebook or grounded-theory approaches: say whether the last excerpt still produced new codes.] Do not go beyond what the text says. Excerpts: [paste text]
```

### Check

Every quoted passage appears word for word in the excerpts and you can name at least one code you would define differently. In reflexive thematic analysis, treat the output as a prompt for your own thinking, not as coding, and do not use saturation as a stopping rule.

## Technology

Paste excerpts into your AI assistant or upload them as Word or PDF files. For your own project, code in dedicated QDA software and use AI only as your ethics approval and data management plan allow.

## Levels

### Chat

Run the starter prompt with the four PDFs attached. Then, in the same chat, give the assistant your revised definition of one code and ask it to recode one paper and list every quote that changed.

### Project / Notebook

Add the four attribution papers (B2, C3, C4, C5) and a Word file with your research question and current coding frame. Ask for every passage that fits one code, with page numbers. For your own study, add only approved, anonymised transcripts as your ethics approval allows. The frame and material stay together as you return – you still check every quote and decision.

### Custom assistant

Create a configured assistant, for example "Qualitative coding helper" (e.g. a Copilot agent, custom GPT, Gemini Gem or Claude project). Add your coding frame as a knowledge file (if your tool does not support knowledge files, attach it in the chat). In the standing instructions, state your approach (e.g. codebook thematic analysis) and the rules: quote word for word with page or line, stay within the text, flag ambiguous passages, never invent quotes or themes. Starter prompt: "Code this excerpt with my frame and flag every borderline passage." Reusable for each new document – only approved, anonymised material goes in, and you check every coded segment.
